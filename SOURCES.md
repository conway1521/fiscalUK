# Primary sources for the institutional framing

Sourced 3 September 2026, **verified at source 3 September 2026**. Supports `RESULTS.md` §7.4 and
`PAPER1.md` §9.

## Verification method

Every quotation below was checked against the raw document text, not against a summary. Hansard
pages were fetched from `api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard`, stripped of markup and string-matched;
Budget 2010 was parsed from the published PDF with `pypdf`; GOV.UK pages were fetched and
string-matched. One page that blocks direct access was verified through the Internet Archive, noted
below.

Status key: **[V]** verified in the source text. **[X]** could not be verified; do not use.

## 1. The 1993 unified Budget (break of +0.288, z 6.96)

### 1.1 Announced, 10 March 1992

**[V]** Norman Lamont, Budget Statement, **HC Deb 10 March 1992, vol 205, c745**:

> The time has come for reform. I therefore intend that next year's Budget will be the last spring
> Budget. From then on the annual Budget will be in December, and it will cover not just taxation but
> also public expenditure.

**[V]** Same column, immediately following:

> The 1994 Finance Bill will be presented to the House in January rather than April. I am publishing
> today a White Paper on the mechanics of this change.

The Finance Bill sentence is worth quoting in the paper. The reform moved the legislative timetable
as well as the announcement, which is the mechanism by which lead times lengthen.

**[X] The White Paper's command number.** Secondary sources give *Budgetary Reform*, Cm 1867, HMSO,
1992. No command number appears anywhere in the Hansard record of 10 March 1992, and the reference
could not be verified. **Cite the White Paper as published on 10 March 1992 on Lamont's own words,
and omit the command number** unless someone confirms it from the document itself.

### 1.2 Brought forward to November, 16 March 1993

**[V]** Lamont, Budget Statement, **HC Deb 16 March 1993, vol 221, cc169-96**:

> In last year's Budget, I announced a far-reaching reform that was supported, I believe, on all
> sides of the House. From November this year, public expenditure and taxation will be brought
> together in one annual Budget statement.

### 1.3 First unified Budget, 30 November 1993

**[V]** Kenneth Clarke delivered it: **HC Deb 30 November 1993, vol 233, cc921-41**.

**CORRECTION, and this matters.** An earlier draft of this file implied the "first unified Budget"
characterisation could be sourced to that sitting's Budget speech. It cannot. **Clarke's statement
never uses the word "unified"**, and the phrase appears in the subsequent debate, spoken by
**Rhodri Morgan**, an opposition backbencher:

> After all, it is an historic Budget. It is the first unified Budget this century, I believe, or for
> half a century at least. Therefore, not only is it unified, but it is four months earlier than most
> other Budgets have been.

Do not attribute that line to Clarke or to the Government. Cite the date and the Chancellor for the
fact; cite Lamont's 1992 and 1993 statements for the reform.

### 1.4 Reverted to spring, 2 July 1997

**[V]** Gordon Brown, Budget Statement, **HC Deb 2 July 1997, vol 297, cc303-16**:

> my next Budget, which I have decided will be in the spring of 1998

This is the candidate the break tests **reject** (+0.026, z 0.36, p 0.72). The Budget moved back to
spring and the long-notice share did not fall back, which is evidence the 1993 step was not a pure
calendar effect.

## 2. The 2010-11 tax policy making reforms (break of +0.257, z 3.20)

### 2.1 Budget 2010

**[V]** HM Treasury, *Budget 2010*, June 2010, HC 61, **paragraph 1.64** (verified by parsing the
published PDF):

> Tax competitiveness is not just about rates and incidence of tax. Predictability, stability and
> simplicity are also important. Alongside this Budget, the Government is publishing proposals to
> improve the way tax policy is made to support these objectives.

The same paragraph confirms the intention to create an independent Office of Tax Simplification.
**[V]** Footnote 21 of that document cites:

> *Tax policy making: a new approach*, HM Treasury and HMRC, June 2010

### 2.2 The consultation response, 9 December 2010

**[V]** David Gauke, Exchequer Secretary to the Treasury, written ministerial statement, *Tax Policy
Making and Draft Finance Bill 2011*, **HC Deb 9 December 2010**:

> At the June Budget, the Government set out their commitment to build a new approach to tax policy
> making, one founded on predictability, stability and simplicity, with consultation on policy and
> scrutiny of legislation as the cornerstones.

and, on lead times:

> Confirming the majority of intended tax changes at least three months ahead of publication of the
> Bill supports predictability in the tax system and provides an opportunity for draft legislation to
> be properly scrutinised.

*Access note:* `publications.parliament.uk` returns HTTP 403 to direct requests. Verified against the
Internet Archive capture of that page (snapshot 26 August 2025). The quotation should be confirmed
against Hansard in a library before submission, though the archived text is the parliamentary record.

### 2.3 The Tax Consultation Framework, 31 March 2011

**[V]** HMRC, *Publication of the Finance (No. 3) Bill and new Tax Consultation Framework*,
31 March 2011:

> Today's Bill is the first to reflect the principles set out in the accompanying document, Tax
> policy making: a new approach. In line with the government's commitment to restoring the UK tax
> system's reputation for predictability, and improving scrutiny, draft legislation for 50 out of 91
> of the Bill's clauses was published for consultation.

## 3. The single fiscal event (rejected as a break: +0.108, p 0.23)

**[V]** HM Treasury, *Autumn Statement 2016*, tax chapter:

> To promote certainty and simplicity within the tax system, the government intends to move towards
> having a single major fiscal event each year. Following the spring 2017 Budget and Finance Bill,
> Budgets will be delivered in the autumn, with the first one taking place in autumn 2017.

**[V]** On the rationale:

> This will improve both external and Parliamentary scrutiny of proposed tax measures.

Use the fuller first quotation. "Certainty and simplicity" is the stated objective and it is precisely
the paper's point: the objective is administrative, not macroeconomic.

## 4. The Treasury's own lead-time target

**[V]** HM Treasury and HMRC, *The new Budget timetable and the tax policy making process*,
6 December 2017:

> stakeholders will continue to be given a minimum of 8 weeks to comment on draft legislation. This
> means that in the new cycle, most policies will be announced at least 16 months before they come
> into effect at the start of the next tax year.

**[V]** And:

> A single autumn Budget will mean tax changes are announced well in advance of the start of the tax
> year in which they will take effect. There will be more time available to scrutinise draft tax
> legislation ahead of its introduction.

This is the strongest citation in the set. It states an intended lead time, in months, for the object
the paper measures. Sixteen months is far beyond the 120-day threshold, consistent with a 2010s
long-notice share of 0.646.

---

## 5. What the sources establish for the framing

Across 1992, 2010, 2016 and 2017 the stated rationales are one family: **parliamentary and external
scrutiny, predictability, stability, certainty, simplicity, and time for taxpayers and stakeholders
to comment.**

Not one is macroeconomic. No document argues about stabilisation, about the anticipation content of
the fiscal impulse, or about households unable to act on advance notice.

The paper's framing follows: lead times lengthened through deliberate reforms of the Budget process,
pursued for scrutiny and legal certainty, whose macroeconomic consequences appear in none of the
documents making the case for them. **Not** "by accident" (retracted, `RESULTS.md` R7), and **not** a
claim that nobody chose the reforms, because they plainly did.
