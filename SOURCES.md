# Primary sources for the institutional framing

Sourced 3 September 2026 for RESULTS.md §7.4. The break-dating exercise puts steps at 1993 and
2010; this file establishes what actually happened on those dates, from Hansard and from HM
Treasury and HMRC documents.

**Verification status.** Every quotation below was retrieved from the cited primary source in this
session, but through an automated fetch that summarises the page. **Check each quotation against the
original before it goes into a submitted draft.** Items marked UNVERIFIED were not confirmed against
a primary source at all and must be chased.

## 1. The 1993 unified Budget (the larger break, +0.288, z 6.96)

**Announced 10 March 1992.** Norman Lamont, Budget Statement, HC Deb, 10 March 1992:

> I therefore intend that next year's Budget will be the last spring Budget. From then on the annual
> Budget will be in December.

and, the same day:

> I am publishing today a White Paper on the mechanics of this change.

- UNVERIFIED: the White Paper is cited in secondary sources as *Budgetary Reform*, Cm 1867, HMSO,
  1992. The command number has not been confirmed against a primary source.

**Brought forward to November, 16 March 1993.** Lamont, Budget Statement, HC Deb, 16 March 1993:

> From November this year, public expenditure and taxation will be brought together in one annual
> Budget statement.

**First unified Budget: 30 November 1993**, delivered by Kenneth Clarke (HC Deb, 30 November 1993),
Lamont having left office in May 1993.

- Note for drafting: Clarke's speech opens on his being new to the office, not on the procedural
  reform. Do not attribute a claim about the unified Budget to that speech without checking it.

**Reverted to spring, 2 July 1997.** Gordon Brown, Budget Statement, HC Deb, 2 July 1997:

> my next Budget, which I have decided will be in the spring of 1998

> There is no spending round this autumn, and there will be no Budget until next spring.

This matters because the 1997 reversion is the one candidate the break tests **reject**
(+0.026, z 0.36, p 0.72). The reform moved the Budget back to spring but the long-gap share did not
fall back, which is evidence that the 1993 step was not purely a calendar effect.

## 2. The 2010-11 tax policy making reforms (the second break, +0.257, z 3.20)

**Budget 2010, June 2010 (HC 61), paragraph 1.64:**

> Tax competitiveness is not just about rates and incidence of tax. Predictability, stability and
> simplicity are also important. Alongside this Budget, the Government is publishing proposals to
> improve the way tax policy is made to support these objectives.

The same paragraph confirms the intention to create an independent Office of Tax Simplification.
Footnote 21 of that Budget cites the accompanying document:

> *Tax policy making: a new approach*, HM Treasury and HMRC, June 2010

**Response to consultation:** *The new approach to tax policy making: a response to the
consultation*, 9 December 2010.

**Tax Consultation Framework:** published in draft December 2010, final version 31 March 2011,
alongside the Finance (No. 3) Bill, of which draft legislation for 50 of 91 clauses had been
published for consultation.

- UNVERIFIED: the commitment to confirm most tax changes "at least three months ahead of publication
  of the Bill" appears in a written ministerial statement of 9 December 2010 (Tax Policy Making and
  Draft Finance Bill 2011). The Hansard page returned 403 and the wording has not been read at
  source.

## 3. The single fiscal event (rejected as a break: +0.108, z 1.20, p 0.23)

**Autumn Statement 2016**, tax chapter:

> the government intends to move towards having a single major fiscal event each year

Rationale as stated:

> This will improve both external and Parliamentary scrutiny of proposed tax measures.

Timetable: a final spring Budget and Finance Bill in 2017, Budgets in autumn from autumn 2017, and
an OBR spring forecast with a Spring Statement from spring 2018. First Spring Statement,
13 March 2018.

## 4. The best citation in the set

**HM Treasury and HMRC, *The new Budget timetable and the tax policy making process*, 6 December
2017:**

> most policies will be announced at least 16 months before they come into effect at the start of
> the next tax year

and an autumn Budget ensures that

> tax changes are announced well in advance of the start of the tax year in which they will take
> effect

**Why this matters.** It is an official statement of an intended lead time, in months, for the
object Paper 1 measures. The paper does not have to infer that lead times lengthened as a
by-product: the Treasury states the target. Sixteen months is far beyond the paper's 120-day
threshold, which is consistent with the 2010s long-gap share of 0.65.

The stated cycle is itself the mechanism: announcement at the autumn Budget, consultation over
winter and spring, draft legislation published in July for eight weeks of technical consultation,
Finance Bill in the autumn, Royal Assent before the tax year begins.

## 5. What the sources establish for the framing

Across 1992, 2010, 2016 and 2017 the stated rationales are consistent, and they are all one family:
**parliamentary and external scrutiny, predictability, stability, simplicity, and giving taxpayers
time to prepare.**

Not one of them is macroeconomic. No document in this set argues about the consequences for
stabilisation, for the anticipation content of the fiscal impulse, or for households who cannot act
on advance notice.

That is the paper's claim, and it is now documented rather than asserted. The framing should be that
lead times lengthened through deliberate reforms pursued for scrutiny and legal certainty, whose
macroeconomic consequences were never part of the case made for them. **Not** "by accident", which
§7.4 already retracted, and **not** a claim that nobody chose the reforms, because they plainly did.
